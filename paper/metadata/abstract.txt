General-purpose tokenizers may allocate vocabulary inefficiently for specialized technical documentation, inflating sequence length. We present a controlled, CPU-only case study of how the training corpus changes byte-level BPE tokenizer compression on unseen documentation, holding the algorithm, pre-tokenizer, decoder, normalization, special tokens, vocabulary size, and training-byte budget fixed. Under a byte-matched 32 MiB budget, we train General, Azure-documentation, and 50/50 Mixed tokenizers at 8K/16K/32K vocabularies and evaluate on nine leakage-controlled held-out sets. On an unseen cross-service Azure holdout (five whole held-out services, 964 documents), the Azure tokenizer uses 36.68% fewer tokens per 1,000 UTF-8 bytes than the byte-matched General tokenizer at 16K (95% paired-bootstrap CI [35.77%, 37.62%]; document win rate 1.000). Full specialization carries a general-English regression of -34.75%; a 50/50 Mixed tokenizer retains +34.21% of the Azure gain with only -4.39% general-English regression. The advantage is stable across vocabulary sizes, persists on prose-dominant content, and transfers partially to other Microsoft and external (Kubernetes) technical documentation. We report compression only: no model-quality, inference-latency, GPU-throughput, or cost claim is made. Code, source manifests with pinned commits, per-document result rows, and a machine-verified claim registry are released; raw corpora are not redistributed.
